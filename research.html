Advanced HVAC Control

Differentiable Predictive Control (DPC) for Multi-zone VAV System (2025 PNNL Summer Internship)

Overview

This study demonstrates physics-informed differentiable predictive control for supply-air-temperature reset in a reference office building with stochastic occupancy. A controller trained on a single HVAC zone was evaluated across five zones on the same floor and then deployed without retraining across all 15 zones in the building, balancing occupant comfort and HVAC energy use.

Key Results
  • Five-zone deployment during the representative cooling week: 54.8% fewer occupied comfort violations and 21.3% lower total HVAC energy use compared with rule-based control strategies.
  • Whole-building zero-shot deployment across 15 zones during the cooling season: 39% fewer comfort violations and 14% lower total HVAC energy use.
Reference

A Whole-Building Demonstration of Differentiable Predictive Control with Stochastic Occupant Behavior

Juwan Ha, Ammar H.A. Dehwah, Karthikeya Devaprasad, and Michael Poplawski

SimBuild 2026 — 12th National Conference of IBPSA-USA, Minneapolis, Minnesota, May 20–22, 2026 (Best Paper in Controls).

Differentiable Predictive Control (DPC) for Lighting System Control (2026 PNNL Summer Internship)

Overview

This ongoing research investigates occupancy-based lighting control, with the goal of reducing unnecessary lighting operation while maintaining lighting availability when spaces are occupied. The modeling and evaluation framework examines stochastic occupancy, passive infrared (PIR) motion-sensor signals, and the trade-off between false-on events (energy waste) and false-off events (lighting comfort violations).

Occupancy and Motion Sensor Modeling

Ground-truth occupant counts are generated by a stochastic occupancy simulator at five-minute intervals and converted to binary presence signals. PIR sensors are modeled using motion events associated with changes in occupant count, followed by a hold time. This distinguishes actual occupancy presence from the motion signal available to the controller.

False-off Events

Motion sensors can indicate an unoccupied space even while people remain present. This occurs when occupants are relatively still, such as during seated office work or long meetings. The illustrated examples show false-off intervals in an open office and a conference room, highlighting the limitations of using motion detection alone for lighting control.

Lighting Control Evaluation

The presented comparisons examine scheduled lighting and PIR-based operation with different hold-time settings, including an optimal hold-time case. A short hold time can reduce unnecessary lighting but increase false-off events; a longer hold time maintains lighting through gaps in motion detection but can increase false-on operation after occupants leave.

The space-type comparison evaluates false-on and false-off durations in open offices, private offices, conference rooms, and other spaces. These results motivate occupancy-aware predictive control that accounts for differences in occupant behavior and space use, rather than relying on one fixed sensor hold time.

Occupancy-based Supervisory Control for Integrated Lighting and HVAC Systems (2026 PNNL Summer Internship)

Overview

This ongoing study investigates occupancy-based supervisory control that integrates lighting and HVAC operation. Differentiable predictive control combines occupancy prediction, lighting on/off and hold-time decisions, supply-air-temperature control, and occupied standby operation to balance energy use with lighting and thermal comfort.

Control Framework

Stochastic room-level occupancy is used to generate PIR sensor data and predict occupied or unoccupied states. The framework combines these signals with a Neural ODE zone-temperature prediction model and a cooling-energy prediction model. Lighting and HVAC controls are evaluated together in an EnergyPlus–Python co-simulation environment.

Case Study
  • Location: Portland, Oregon, climate zone 4C (mixed marine).
  • Building: a high-fidelity medium-sized office model with rooftop-unit-based multi-zone VAV systems.
  • Evaluation period: the cooling season, May through September.
  • Comparisons: scheduled control, outdoor-air-temperature reset, ASHRAE Guideline 36, PIR-based occupancy control, and integrated DPC with an optimal hold time.
Energy and Comfort Results

In the presented simulation, integrated DPC uses 15,462 kWh compared with 23,509 kWh for the schedule-based baseline, a 34.2% reduction. The comparison includes cooling, fan, reheat, and lighting energy.

The comfort comparison shows why energy use must be evaluated alongside occupancy sensing errors. Some occupancy-based settings reduce energy use but substantially increase thermal or lighting comfort violations. Integrated DPC maintains low violation levels while reducing energy use; these preliminary results are specific to the presented case study.

Grid-interactive Efficient Buildings (GEBs)

AI-Tech Assistant (2024 LBNL Summer Internship)

Overview

This research develops an AI-Tech Assistant to support the deployment of low-cost model predictive control (MPC) in small and medium commercial buildings. By adjusting HVAC operation, MPC can reduce peak demand and shift electricity use. The study focuses on making the knowledge needed to implement this technology more accessible to engineers and other stakeholders.

Supporting Technology Deployment

Deploying MPC requires more than a control algorithm: practitioners also need to assess site suitability, install the system, and configure its operation. The assistant provides conversational guidance on these tasks, helping users navigate deployment procedures and troubleshoot issues without relying on repeated exchanges with research experts.

Assistant Design and Evaluation

The prototype combines a large language model with deployment-specific instructions, technical context, and custom function tools. Example scenarios examine how it explains MPC concepts, checks site eligibility, and generates configuration templates, while keeping responses within the intended technical scope. These demonstrations illustrate its potential to support technology transfer and identify directions for further development.

Data Center

Liquid-Cooled AI Data Centers

Overview

We aim to develop a high-fidelity, physics-based Modelica modeling framework for liquid-cooled data centers (LCDC) to address the large thermal loads generated by high-density AI server racks. While existing Modelica-based data center models have largely been limited to air-cooled system configurations, this study expands the modeling scope to comprehensively represent both the Facility Water System (FWS) and the Technology Cooling System (TCS) of LCDCs in accordance with the ASHRAE Liquid Cooling Guideline. In particular, to reflect the allowable operating temperature ranges of liquid-cooled server racks, appropriate FWS configurations are established based on the TCS fluid classes defined in the guideline. These configurations include chiller/cooling tower systems, water-side economizers (WSE), and dry coolers, and are classified into the following cases:

  • LCDC - Case A: Chiller Only / WSE Partial Cooling Mode
  • LCDC - Case B: Cooling Tower Only
  • LCDC - Case C: Dry Cooler Only

On the TCS side, the modeling framework incorporates major liquid-cooling components, including Coolant Distribution Units (CDUs), direct-to-chip liquid cooling, rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx), and immersion cooling. This framework is expected to support warmer facility water operation and longer free-cooling hours, thereby improving overall cooling system efficiency and reducing cooling energy use by approximately 20-30%.

2-1) IT and Cooling Load Modeling

Figure 3 presents the overall framework for IT and cooling load modeling. We define the cooling load of our data center models based on tray-level rated IT power for a 2 CPU and 4 GPU configuration (e.g., 6.7 kW) and rack-level rated power (e.g., 120 kW), which are broadly representative of AI training and inference services. Using these rated values, auxiliary loads beyond the servers, and a design-rated Cooling Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE, Total Energy/IT Energy) of 1.2, we estimate the rated IT and cooling loads for a 1 GW-class liquid-cooled data center (LCDC).

We then generate stochastic tray-level IT workloads at the millisecond scale and aggregate them to the rack and Coolant Distribution Unit (CDU) levels. At the cooling interface, direct-to-chip cooling removes heat directly from the primary compute components, while residual rack-level air-side heat is captured through rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx).

These aggregated IT workloads are then translated into minute-resolution cooling loads by accounting for the thermal inertia of the liquid-cooling loop, fluid transport delay, and the response characteristics of the cooling control system. Because the cooling infrastructure responds more slowly than the millisecond-scale IT workload, the resulting plant-level demand is naturally smoothed over time. The resulting composite load curve therefore captures not only the IT load itself, but also cooling tower fans, condenser-water-system (CWS) pumps, chillers, facility-water-system (FWS) pumps, technology-cooling-system (TCS) pumps, Coolant Distribution Units (CDUs), RDHx fans, and other relevant auxiliary loads, enabling a smoother and more physically realistic representation of the coupled IT-cooling dynamics.

Figure 3. IT and Cooling Load Modeling

2-2) Liquid Cooling Data Center System

On the TCS side, the coolant distribution unit (CDU) is modeled to represent a hybrid cooling architecture in which heat generated by the primary compute components, such as CPUs and GPUs, is removed directly through cold-plate-based direct-to-chip cooling, while residual rack-level air-side heat is captured through active rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx). This enables a more realistic representation of the combined liquid-cooling and air-side supplemental cooling characteristics of AI data centers.

At Data Hall level, each system is designed with N+1 redundancy to ensure stable and reliable operation, and the potential application of thermal energy storage tanks is also considered to enhance emergency response capability and provide additional operational flexibility.

Real-world Dataset for LCDC Model Validation

M100 dataset 1 is a real-world open dataset collected from Marconi100, a Tier-0 supercomputer at CINECA, and can serve as a valuable reference dataset for validating LCDC (Liquid Cooling Data Center) models. It includes not only node-level measurements from more than 980 computing nodes, such as core load, temperature, frequency, memory activity, CPU power, fan speed, and GPU utilization, but also system-level information covering the liquid cooling infrastructure, air-conditioning system, power supply units, workload manager statistics, job-related information, system alerts, and weather data. Covering the period from March to December 2020, the dataset is stored as a partitioned Parquet dataset organized by year_month, plugin, and metric, and distributed as monthly tarballs, making it well suited for analyzing the thermal and operational behavior of large-scale liquid-cooled data centers and for model validation purposes.

GIS (Raleigh City)

Overview

City-scale 3D building models of Raleigh, North Carolina, organized by Level of Detail (LoD):

  • LOD 1 – block models: each building footprint extruded to a single roof height.
  • LOD 2 – building models with roof shapes.
  • LOD 3 – detailed building models with facade elements such as windows.

GIS (Raleigh City)

LOD 1

Raleigh LoD1 3D Building Model

A Level-of-Detail 1 (LoD1) block model of every building within the City of Raleigh (about 156,000 buildings). Each building footprint is extruded to its roof height measured from the 2024 QL1 airborne LiDAR; where LiDAR heights were unavailable (about 11% of buildings), heights are estimated from floor counts. The model serves as the geometric base for city-scale building energy modeling.
